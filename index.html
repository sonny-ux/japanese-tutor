Japanese Language

§ 1

How Japanese Works

Before vocabulary lists or conjugation tables, the most important thing is understanding the architecture of the language.

Word Order — SOV

English is SVO: Subject → Verb → Object. "I drink coffee." Japanese is SOV: Subject → Object → Verb. わたしは　コーヒーを　のみます。 The verb comes last, always. This isn't just a quirk — it shapes how sentences are built, how clauses nest inside each other, and why relative clauses in Japanese come before the noun they modify (the opposite of English).

Word order is actually quite flexible in Japanese, because particles do the work that position does in English. Particles are small grammatical suffixes that attach to nouns and tell you what role that noun plays in the sentence — subject, object, destination, tool, and so on. Once you internalize this, Japanese starts to feel logical rather than backwards.

Topic vs. Subject — は vs. が

This is the distinction that confuses learners most. は (wa) marks the topic — it sets what the sentence is about, often something already known or established. が (ga) marks the subject — the grammatical actor performing the action, often with a sense of emphasis or new information.

Consider: わたしはがくせいです (watashi wa gakusei desu, "I am a student") vs. わたしがやった！ (watashi ga yatta!, "I'm the one who did it!"). The は version states a fact about the established topic "me." The が version emphasizes who did it — it's contrastive or identifying.

In many sentences は implies が. "As for me, [I] am a student." The subject is understood. But when you want to specify WHO or WHAT — when context demands precision — use が. Also: verbs of desire (ほしい), ability (できる), and like/dislike (すきだ) always take が for their object, not を. すしがすきです — "I like sushi."

Key Principle は sets the frame ("as for X…"). が specifies the actor or identifies ("X is the one…"). A sentence can have both: ぞうは　はなが　ながい — "As for elephants, [their] noses are long." ぞう is the topic; はな is the subject of the predicate.

How Sentences Build

Japanese sentences build from left to right, with modifiers always coming before what they modify. Adjectives precede nouns. Relative clauses precede nouns. Time and location phrases come early. The predicate — verb, adjective, or noun + copula — comes last and carries tense. Negation and politeness are expressed entirely through verb endings; there's no auxiliary like "do" or "did."

§ 2

The "To Be" Verbs — ある, いる, だ/です

Japanese has no single "to be." The concept splits into three distinct words depending on what you're expressing.

English "is/are/am" conflates existence with identity. Japanese separates them: ある (aru) and いる (iru) express physical existence (there is, there are), while だ / です express equation or description (X is Y, X is [adjective]).

ある — Existence of Inanimate Things

Use ある for things that don't move on their own: objects, places, abstract things, events. "The book is on the desk." "There is a park." "There is a meeting tomorrow." The location takes に; the existing thing takes が.

つくえのうえに
location · に
ほんが　あります。
book · が · aru·polite

Tsukue no ue ni hon ga arimasu. — "There is a book on the desk."

いる — Existence of Animate Things

Use いる for people, animals, and anything that moves of its own will. "My friend is at the station." "There are two cats." A robot or doll uses ある (inanimate); a pet fish uses いる (animate). Same sentence structure: location に, existing thing が, then いる/います.

えきに
location · に
ともだちが　います。
friend · が · iru·polite

Eki ni tomodachi ga imasu. — "My friend is at the station."

だ / です — Copula (Identity & Description)

Use だ (casual) or です (polite) after nouns and な-adjectives to say "X is Y." This is not about location or existence — it's a statement of identity or category. あれはえんぴつです "That is a pencil." い-adjectives do NOT use だ/です as a copula — they carry their own predicate form.

Common Confusion えきはどこですか？ "Where is the station?" uses です — it's asking for identification/location information. But えきにコンビニがあります "There is a convenience store at the station" uses ある — genuine existence of an object.

§ 3

Verb Conjugation

Japanese verbs are entirely regular once you know which of three groups a verb belongs to. The group determines the conjugation pattern — and the group is almost always predictable from the verb's ending.

The Three Groups

Group 1 — Godan / U-verbs: These verbs end in any consonant + u sound: く、ぐ、す、ぬ、ぶ、む、る (G1 type)、う、つ. The name "godan" (five-level) refers to the fact that the stem vowel cycles through all five vowel rows (a/i/u/e/o) across different conjugations. Example: 書く (kaku) → 書か (a-row, negative), 書き (i-row, polite stem), 書く (u-row, dictionary), 書け (e-row, imperative), 書こう (o-row, volitional).

Group 2 — Ichidan / Ru-verbs: These verbs end in -iru or -eru. The stem never changes — you simply drop る and add endings directly. "Ichidan" (one-level) because the stem stays on one vowel. Example: 食べる (taberu) → stem たべ — add ます: たべます, add ない: たべない, add て: たべて.

Group 3 — Irregular: Only two verbs: する (suru, "to do") and くる (kuru, "to come"). These must be memorized.

The "False Friend" Trap Several common verbs end in -iru or -eru but are actually Group 1 (Godan): 帰る (kaeru, return), 入る (hairu, enter), 知る (shiru, know), 要る (iru, need), 走る (hashiru, run), 切る (kiru, cut). Test: if you're unsure, try the negative. Group 1 verbs form ない by changing to a-row (帰らない), while Group 2 drops る and adds ない (食べない). Misidentifying these gives you wrong forms like 帰りない, which doesn't exist.

The te-form of Group 1 verbs involves sound changes determined by the verb's final syllable. These follow consistent phonetic patterns — the result of historical sound assimilation, not arbitrary rules.

Key Te-Form Combinations

The te-form is not a tense — it's a connector. It links actions (do X and then Y), attaches to auxiliary verbs to build new meanings, and forms the basis of the progressive and many other patterns.

PatternExampleMeaning & Notes
~てみる食べてみるTry doing (tabete miru). みる = "to see," so literally "do and see what happens." Implies willingness to experiment.
~てしまう / ~ちゃう食べてしまった / 食べちゃったEnded up doing; often conveys regret, surprise, or completion. ちゃう is the casual contraction.
~てもいい行ってもいいIt's okay to do; permission. "You may go." Negative: ~てはいけない = must not.
~てはいけない入ってはいけないMust not / prohibited. Casual contraction: ~ちゃいけない.
~ている / ~てる食べている / 食べてるOngoing action (eating right now) OR ongoing state (知っている = "knows" / is in a state of knowing). Context determines which.
~てから食べてからAfter doing X (then Y). "After eating, I studied." The て action is clearly completed before the next one starts.
~てあげる教えてあげるDo as a favor for someone else. The speaker (or in-group) performs the action outward.
~てもらう教えてもらうHave someone do for you — you receive the action. Focus on the beneficiary (speaker).
~てくれる教えてくれるSomeone does for you (action directed inward to speaker/in-group). Focus on the giver's kindness.

§ 5

Adjectives

Japanese adjectives come in two fundamentally different types, and understanding why they're different makes their conjugation predictable rather than arbitrary.

い-adjectives are actually a kind of verb — they have their own inflectional endings and conjugate for tense and negation directly, without needing だ/です as a copula. The い at the end is part of the grammatical machinery encoding present tense. When you say たかい (takai, "expensive"), that い is already doing verbal work.

な-adjectives are essentially nouns that describe. They cannot conjugate on their own — they rely on the copula (だ/です) for tense and negation. When they modify a noun directly, they add な before it. Think of them as noun-ish words that got promoted to adjective duty.

§ 6

Nouns

Japanese nouns don't inflect — no plurals, no cases, no gender. Context and particles do all that work. Numbers require counter suffixes that vary by what's being counted: 枚 (flat things), 本 (long things), 匹 (small animals), 台 (machines), 人 (people). The numbers 1–10 have both native Japanese and Sino-Japanese readings; which you use depends on the counter.

§ 7

Adverbs & Connectors

Japanese adverbs are invariable — they never conjugate. Most can move fairly freely in the sentence, though they typically appear just before the word or phrase they modify. Sentence-level adverbs usually come at the front of the clause.

Frequency

Two frequency adverbs require special attention because they must pair with a negative verb to convey the meaning beginners expect. 全然 (zenzen) means "not at all" — but only when the verb is negative. With an affirmative verb in casual speech, it colloquially means "totally / completely" (a newer usage). A beginner who says 全然わかる has said "I totally understand" — the intended 全然わからない means "I don't understand at all." Similarly, あまり (amari) means "not very much" only with a negative: 全然わからない = "don't understand at all," あまりわからない = "don't understand very well."

§ 8

Casual Speech

Textbook Japanese is polite Japanese — ます and です everywhere. Real spoken Japanese, especially among friends and family, is quite different. Understanding these patterns is essential for listening comprehension and natural interaction.

Core Switches: Polite → Casual

PoliteCasualNotes
ですだCopula. だ is often dropped entirely in questions or soft statements, especially by women.
〜ます / 〜ませんplain form / 〜ないDictionary form for present, した / しなかった for past.
〜ますか？〜の？ / rising intonationの softens questions. Bare rising tone also works in very casual speech.
〜てください〜てTe-form alone = casual request ("please do X").
〜ではありません〜じゃない / 〜じゃんじゃん adds "right?" / assertion flavor (common in Kanto region).

Gendered & Register-Marked Speech

Modern Japanese is losing many gendered speech patterns, especially among younger generations. That said, certain markers remain common, and recognizing them is important for listening comprehension.

Dropped Particles In casual speech, は, が, and を are frequently dropped when the meaning is clear from context. コーヒー飲む？ (koohii nomu?) = "Drink coffee?" — no を, no subject, no politeness. This is normal and sounds natural. Over-using particles in casual conversation can sound stiff or overly formal.
